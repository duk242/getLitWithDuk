This is the "Technically Correct" way to do a front wash. But it requires a lot of things to fall in to place in terms of number of lights available and you can get the angles you need from the positioning of the lighting bars.

The front wash is the foundation of almost every theatrical lighting design. Its primary job is simple but critical: **make sure the audience can see the actors' faces.** 

While you can technically create a front wash with fresnels or par cans, **Profile Lights (like the Nitec Pharos)** are the industry standard. They give you absolute control over where the light goes, allow you to use shutters to cut light off the proscenium arch or set pieces, and let you control the softness of the beam edge.

Here is the step-by-step guide to designing and focusing a flawless front wash.

---

## 1. The Geometry: The McCandless Method

If you just point one light straight at an actor's face, they will look flat and washed out. To create natural-looking shadows and three-dimensionality, lighting designers have relied on the **McCandless Method** for nearly a century.

Instead of one light, you use **two profile lights per acting area**:
* **45 Degrees Up:** The lights should be rigged so they hit the actor at a 45-degree vertical angle. (Any steeper, and you get "raccoon eyes" from brow shadows. Any lower, and you blind the actor and flatten their features).
* **45 Degrees Out:** One light comes from 45 degrees to the actor's left, and the other from 45 degrees to their right. 

## 2. Divide the Stage into Zones

You cannot light an entire stage with just two lights. You need to break your stage down into overlapping **Acting Areas** (or "Zones"). 

A standard acting area is about 8 to 10 feet in diameter. For a typical medium-sized stage, you might have:
* 3 zones across the Downstage edge (Down Right, Down Center, Down Left)
* 3 zones across the Midstage line
* 3 zones across the Upstage line

**Total Profile Lights needed:** 9 zones × 2 lights per zone = 18 Profile Lights.

:::note[Planning your Rig]
If you don't have exactly 45-degree hanging positions in your theatre, don't panic! 45 degrees is the *ideal* starting point, but you often have to compromise based on where your FOH (Front of House) bars are located. Get as close as you can.
:::

## 3. Choosing Your Colours

To add depth, designers typically put a **Warm** colour in one side of the wash, and a **Cool** colour in the other. 

* **The Warm Side:** Simulates sunlight or indoor practical lighting. (Common gels: *Rosco 02 Bastard Amber* or *Lee 204 Full CTO*).
* **The Cool Side:** Simulates shadows or ambient sky light. (Common gels: *Rosco 60 No Color Blue* or *Lee 201 Full CTB*).

When both lights hit the actor's face, they mix to create a very natural, flattering white light in the center, with gentle warm and cool shadows wrapping around the sides of their head.

## 4. Focusing the Profiles

Focusing profile lights for a wash requires a specific technique to ensure the stage looks like one continuous pool of light, rather than a bunch of overlapping spotlights.

Follow these steps for every single light in your wash:

1. **Center the Hotspot:** Turn the light on and point it at a person standing exactly in the center of the zone. Put the brightest part of the beam right on their face.
2. **Make it Sharp:** Run the lens barrel back and forth until you have a perfectly sharp, hard edge on the floor. 
3. **Make your Shutter Cuts:** Push the shutters in to cut the light off anything it shouldn't hit. Keep it off the proscenium arch, the front row of the audience, and the teaser curtains.
4. **"Run the Barrel" (Soften the Edge):** Once your cuts are made, slide the lens barrel in or out to blur the edge of the beam. 

:::tip[Pro-Tip: Blending the Wash]
You *must* soften the edge of your profiles for a front wash! If you leave the edges sharp, the audience will see distinct circles of light on the floor, and actors will noticeably step in and out of the light. A soft edge allows Zone A to seamlessly blend into Zone B.
:::

## 5. Balancing the Wash at the Desk

Once all your lights are focused, go to your lighting desk (like your EOS console). Bring up all the Warm lights to 80%, and all the Cool lights to 80%. 

Have someone walk slowly across the stage from left to right, and front to back.
* **Are there dark spots?** You may need to adjust the focus of a light to overlap more with its neighbor.
* **Are there hot spots?** If the area where two zones overlap is too bright, you can use the desk to drop the intensity of specific lights slightly to even it all out.

Once it looks perfectly even, you have your front wash! You can now record this as a baseline cue or Submaster to build the rest of your show upon.
