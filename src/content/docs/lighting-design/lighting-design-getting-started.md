# My Process/Workflow for Design
While this is listed as step by step, it doesn't necessarily go in order. In fact a lot of it will happen all at the same time. You might be working through the script and decide that your front wash might need to be changed, or there's an area of the stage that's not used very much so you can get away with less light there.
1. Collaboration and Vision - Talk to the director about what they envision lighting wise! Do they want a lot of colour and flashy effects or are they aiming for something where the actors are the only thing telling the story and the light is just there so you can see them. Try and work out the vibes before you start doing any planning!
1. Start with what's already there. If there's a default "house rig" setup - use as much of that as you can. This means less work setting up and less work when if you have to put it back to the default state!
2. Work out what you need to do with the script and blocking. This means where people will be on the stage so you can make sure you've got enough lights to hit each area.
3. Work out your front wash. This is so you can see everyones faces. There's a lot of ways to do a decent front wash, here's some common ones:
* The McCandless method which relies on 3 lights at 45º angles (two front + 1 back) to light up faces with both warm and cool white. This is generally regarded as the best practice, but is hardest to get right!
* The "Bunch of lights spaced evenly for a nice even wash" - I shall call it the "Steve Wash", as he was the guy who showed me this method. One bar infront of the stage with your lights hitting everyone at somewhere between 30-60º, evened out so there's no dark spots.
* Spots Only - This is more useful if you're lighting a band, and they're not moving around much. One or two spots per band member. This is more about making it so you can see their faces, but the real magic comes from the effect lighting. That being said - I usually have lights setup so I can cover broad areas of the stage incase they decide to run around!
4. Add in colour - This is your main weapon to convey emotion and to help set the scene. Whether it's just using Cyc lights to light up the backdrop into a nice sunset, LED PARs with a dark blue to show night time or sadness. It'll help shape the mood!
5. Specials - When you want to light someone up, but don't want to blast whole sections of the stage, you can use a Profile pointing at a particular place on stage or a followspot to highlight them.
6. Once I've worked out what sort of lights I need - I'll work on the design documentation and lighting diagram. This is where I have what lights go where, what DMX Addresses they're using, where they're pointing and gels (if needed).

## Other things to think about as you're designing
* Will there be haze?  
This will change how you use many of the lights. Without haze you're relying on the light reflecting off things for people to see it. With Haze you can use the beams of light in the air.  Haze makes everything better!
* Will there be a followspot - or a moving light that can be used as a follow spot?
If you're using a followspot - you can use that as a cheatcode for your specials - it's a great way to single one person out on stage whilst getting minimal amount of light spilling around them.  You can use it instead of setting up a bunch of profiles on stage pointing at very specific positions.
Just note that the angle you're hitting them from will be different vs a profile hung right near them!
* Access to the lights - Some venues it's super easy to get to the lighting bar to hang/focus lights. Other venues you'll need to hire a ladder or scissorlift to reach them.
